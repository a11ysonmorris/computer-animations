Enhancements I made

Enhancement 01

Planet textures

I replaced the default planet materials with higher-resolution textures from solarsystemscope.com, giving each planet realistic surface color and detail instead of flat shading.

Enhancement 02

Sun as the light source

Using Maya's illumination settings, I set the sun as the scene's light source so each planet is lit and shadowed the way it would be from actual sunlight.

Enhancement 03

Planet position and speed

I adjusted the position and orbit speed of each planet so their spacing and motion better reflect their real relative distances and speeds in the solar system.

Modified animation

Playblast v2 — the final animation with all three enhancements applied.

Notes

The textures made the biggest visual difference. By swapping in real planetary surface maps, immediately the scene felt less like a placeholder and more like an actual solar system. The process of switching the light source to the sun took a bit of trial and error in Maya to get the shadows falling naturally on each planet, but it was worth it for how much more physically correct the lighting looks. Finally, adjusting the position and speed of the planets last tied everything together and made the orbits feel more intentional.

Modeling & Shading

Modeling: I built the treasure chest in Maya using polygonal modeling techniques to create the main box, curved lid, and smaller details. I used extrusion and beveling to shape the chest and refine its edges, then adjusted the geometry to create the rounded top and overall proportions. I also modeled the coins, gems, and pearls separately so they could be placed inside the chest.

Shading: I used separate materials for the wooden chest, metal hardware, coins, gems, and pearls to distinguish the different surfaces. I adjusted the materials and lighting to give the objects more depth and detail, and used smooth shading to make the curved parts of the chest and the treasure appear more polished.

Treasure: The treasure was modeled without downloading treasure models from the internet. The final chest contains coins, gems, and pearls.

Project Notes

This project gave me more experience building a complete object from scratch in Maya and thinking about how separate pieces work together as one model. One of the main challenges was creating the curved lid while keeping the shape smooth and consistent with the rest of the chest. I also had to make sure the chest could open correctly and that the treasure stayed inside during the animation. Adding different materials and smooth shading helped give the final model more detail and made the different parts of the chest and treasure easier to distinguish.

Project Bouncy Ball

Storyboard & Animation Description

My animation follows a bouncing ball as it moves through the scene. The ball interacts with its surroundings and uses changes in speed, direction, and shape to make the movement feel natural and expressive.

The animation focuses on realistic, physics-based movement while incorporating squash and stretch, timing, and other principles of animation to give the ball personality.

Storyboard 1 — six-panel shot breakdown. View on Canva

Storyboard 2 — ideation, shot creation, and visual inspiration board. View on Canva

Disney Principles of Animation

The following principles are demonstrated in my animation. Each helps make the ball's movement more believable and visually interesting.

The ball squashing and stretching mid-fall.

Principle 01

Squash and Stretch

The ball changes shape as it hits the ground and rebounds. It squashes on impact and stretches as it moves through the air, emphasizing the force and speed of the movement.

The ball mid-jump, trying to make it over the tree.

Principle 02

Anticipation

Each time the ball jumps at the tree, it builds anticipation because we don't know whether it will make it over. The repeated attempts keep the viewer waiting to see if the next jump will finally work.

The ball sweating and moving slowly before it sees the water.

Principle 03

Timing and Spacing

At the start, the ball moves slowly because it is tired and has no water. Once it spots the puddle, its timing speeds up and it moves faster, jumping with excitement as it tries to reach the water.

I used materials and lighting in Maya to give the ball a more finished appearance and help it stand out from the environment. The shading adds depth to the scene and makes the ball easier to follow throughout the animation.

Project Notes

This project helped me understand how much personality and emotion can come from animating even a simple object. I learned how to use timing, anticipation, and squash and stretch to make movement feel more expressive, rather than just physically accurate. I also explored how to guide the viewer's attention and build toward a payoff through movement and pacing. One of my biggest takeaways was how much small details can change the way an animation feels and how important it is to think about the story behind the movement, not just the movement itself.

Animation Description

My Rube Goldberg machine takes place in a vintage kitchen in the morning. An alarm clock goes off and starts a chain reaction across the counter that ends with the toaster popping up the toast.

Storyboard

1. The alarm clock rings, tumbling down onto the wood block and bumping the orange.

2. The orange now begins to roll down the cutting board, which is propped up by a cheese grater.

3. The orange reaches the bottom of the cutting board, bumping into the pepper grinder.

4. The pepper grinder crashes into the rolling pin, which quickly begins to roll.

5. The rolling pin creates a domino effect with the mini cereal boxes, milk, and cookbook.

6. The cookbook hits the toaster lever and the toast pops, with the toast landing on a nearby plate.

Project 5 – Domino Reveal

Name Allyson Morris
Date 11/18/2026
Project Python Scripting

Animation Description

For this project, I wrote a Python script in Maya that builds a grid of dominoes and knocks them over to reveal an image. When the script runs, it asks the user to choose an image and uses the image's dimensions to decide how many rows and columns of dominoes to create. Each domino is then colored on one face to match the part of the image it sits on. Because the dominoes start out a plain ivory color, the image stays hidden until they fall and the colored faces land facing up.

The camera begins low beside the dominoes as the chain reaction starts and then pulls up to an overhead view, so the full image can be seen once everything has fallen. Everything in the scene, from the dominoes to the floor, lights, and camera, is created by the script.

How the Script Works

Step 01

Choosing the image

The script begins by clearing the scene and opening a file dialog so the user can choose an image. It then reads the image's width and height and uses them to figure out how many rows and columns of dominoes to make, while making sure there are always at least 200 dominoes. 

fileResult = cmds.fileDialog2(fileMode=1, caption='Pick an image for the dominoes to reveal')

inputFile = cmds.shadingNode('file', asTexture=True, name='revealImage')
cmds.setAttr(inputFile + '.fileTextureName', fileResult[0], type='string')
imgW = cmds.getAttr(inputFile + '.outSizeX')
imgH = cmds.getAttr(inputFile + '.outSizeY')

numCols = NUM_COLS
numRows = int(round(numCols * (SPACING_X / SPACING_Z) * float(imgH) / imgW))
while numCols * numRows < 200:
    numCols += 2
    numRows = int(round(numCols * (SPACING_X / SPACING_Z) * float(imgH) / imgW))

Step 02

Setting up the grid with MASH

I used a MASH network with a grid layout to place the dominoes. Rather than simulating the MASH dominoes directly, the script takes the position of each domino from the network and creates a real domino in that same spot. 

mashNetwork = mapi.Network()
mashNetwork.createNetwork(name="Dominos")

cmds.setAttr(mashNetwork.distribute + '.arrangement', 6)   # grid
cmds.setAttr(mashNetwork.distribute + '.gridx', numCols)
cmds.setAttr(mashNetwork.distribute + '.gridz', numRows)
cmds.setAttr(mashNetwork.distribute + '.gridAmplitudeX', sizeX)
cmds.setAttr(mashNetwork.distribute + '.gridAmplitudeZ', sizeZ)

mashNetwork.getCurrentFrameData()
positions = list(mashNetwork.position)

Step 03

Coloring the dominoes

Each domino is colored based on the part of the image at its position. Only the face that ends up on top after the domino falls is colored, while the rest of the domino stays ivory. This is what keeps the image hidden at the start. 

for i, p in enumerate(positions):
    x, z = p[0], p[2]
    dom = cmds.duplicate(master, name='domino%03d' % i)[0]
    cmds.move(x, 0.01, z, dom)

    u = (x - xMin) / (xMax - xMin)
    v = (zMax - z) / (zMax - zMin)
    rgb = cmds.colorAtPoint(inputFile, output='RGB', u=u, v=v)

    key = tuple(round((c ** 2.2) * 15) / 15.0 for c in rgb)
    if key not in colorSGs:
        colorSGs[key] = makeShader('pixel%03d' % len(colorSGs), key, flat=True)
    cmds.sets(dom + '.f[5]', e=True, forceElement=colorSGs[key])

Step 04

Making them fall

The dominoes and the floor are set up as rigid bodies with gravity. To start the chain reaction, an invisible log moves into the first domino of every row at the same time. The script then runs the simulation by stepping through every frame of the animation. 

for dom in dominoes:
    cmds.rigidBody(dom, active=True, mass=1.0, staticFriction=0.8, dynamicFriction=0.6)

cmds.setKeyframe(roller, attribute='translateX', time=1, value=startX)
cmds.setKeyframe(roller, attribute='translateX', time=ROLLER_HIT_FRAME, value=hitX)
cmds.rigidBody(roller, passive=True)
cmds.setAttr(roller + '.visibility', 0)

cmds.gravity(name='dominoGravity', magnitude=9.8, directionY=-1)
cmds.connectDynamic(dominoes, fields='dominoGravity')

for f in range(1, END_FRAME + 1):
    cmds.currentTime(f)

Step 05

Camera, lights, and output

Finally, the script adds the lights and keyframes the camera movement. At the end, a pop-up asks whether to render the animation, playblast it, or skip that step. 

camKeys = [
    (1,   (width * 0.7, 3, depth * 0.6)),                     # low shot
    (90,  (width * 0.3, overheadY * 0.35, depth * 1.1)),
    (200, (0, overheadY, 0.5)),                               # overhead
]

choice = cmds.confirmDialog(title='Domino Reveal', message='Render or playblast?',
                            button=['Render', 'Playblast', 'Neither'])

Struggles

The cylinder going through a domino instead of pushing it.

Struggle 01

MASH dynamics on my laptop

One of the main challenges was that MASH dynamics would not work on my laptop. The dominoes never knocked each other over, and the cylinder from the starter code passed right through them instead of pushing them. I tried running it in Maya 2025, 2026, and 2027, changed the evaluation settings, and loaded the Bullet plug-in, but none of these fixed the problem. Autodesk's documentation notes that MASH dynamics only uses an animated collider's starting position, which matched what I was seeing. In the end, I kept MASH for setting up the grid and switched to Maya's regular rigid bodies for the falling, which worked much more reliably.

Struggle 02

Getting the dominoes to fall

Getting the first dominoes to fall correctly took a lot of trial and error. At one point, the first domino fell backward, away from the rest of the line. Without enough friction, the dominoes also slid across the floor instead of tipping over. Increasing the friction fixed the sliding, and an invisible log moving into the first domino of each row gave the chain reaction a consistent start.

An early test with washed-out colors.

Struggle 03

Making the image readable

In my first tests, the image was difficult to make out. The colors looked washed out, and thin gaps of floor showed between the rows. To fix this, I switched the colored faces to a flat shader that is not affected by the lighting, adjusted how the image colors were converted, and made the dominoes slightly deeper to close the gaps. I also changed the dominoes from black to ivory, since the black edges made the final image look messy.

My test image, the W&M ACM logo.

Struggle 04

Small details and run time

My test image was the W&M ACM logo, but its thin letters and small text were lost because each domino can only show one color. Because of this, I switched to a Mondrian painting, which is made up of large blocks of color. The script also took a long time to run with around 900 dominoes. Using fewer dominoes, shortening the animation, and stopping the viewport from updating while the dominoes were created made it run noticeably faster.

Project Notes

This project showed me how much of an animation can be built through scripting rather than by hand. Most of my time went into debugging, and when the dominoes would not fall, I had to test one piece at a time to figure out whether the problem was my code, the settings, or Maya itself. I also learned how much the choice of image matters. Simple, bold shapes show up much better in dominoes than small details, which is why the Mondrian painting worked so well in the end.
